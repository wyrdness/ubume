## Overview

Ubume (産女, 'birthing woman') is a yōkai of Japanese folklore, classified among the yūrei (ghosts) of women who died during pregnancy or in childbirth. She appears most often at night, beside roads, bridges, or rivers, in the form of a pale, blood-stained woman wearing a red waist cloth and cradling an infant. She approaches travelers and asks them to hold her baby; once the bundle is taken it grows progressively heavier, or transforms on examination into a bundle of leaves, a stone, or a buddha-statue. In other accounts she buys candy at a shop with leaf-money for a child whom locals later find buried with a real living infant in his mother's grave. The Ubume is rarely portrayed as purely malevolent — she is moved by maternal grief and the unfulfilled obligation to her child — and compassion shown to her can lay her spirit to rest. The earliest written account is in the 12th-century Konjaku Monogatarishū, where the warrior Urabe no Suetake encounters an ubume crossing a river. The yōkai's iconography is also strongly shaped by the Chinese-derived bird-spirit Kokakuchō (姑獲鳥), introduced to Japan via texts such as the Wakan Sansai Zue (1712).

## Etymology

- **Origin**: The name 'ubume' is written 産女 ('birthing woman'). It is also written with the Chinese-derived 姑獲鳥 ('kokakuchō'), originally a Chinese bird-spirit absorbed into the Japanese ubume tradition during the medieval period.
- **Meaning**: 'Birthing woman'; the alternative writing 姑獲鳥 carries the sense of a bird-form spirit that snatches children
- **First use**: Earliest extant Japanese attestation: Konjaku Monogatarishū (early 12th century)

## Theories & Explanations

### Paranormal / Cryptozoological

**Onryō / unsettled spirit** _(Japanese folk Buddhist tradition)_

Within Japanese folk Buddhism, ubume are real spirits of women whose violent or unsatisfied deaths bind them to the world until proper rites (especially mizuko kuyō for the lost infant) release them.

### Skeptical

**Cultural processing of maternal mortality** _(Folklorists Komatsu Kazuhiko and others)_

Ubume folklore encodes pre-modern Japan's high maternal and infant mortality rates and the cultural anxiety around women dying in childbirth.

**Hybrid with Chinese bird-spirit** _(Kabat, A.; Foster, M.D.)_

Many of the more elaborate ubume features (bird-form, eerie flames, gull-like behavior) are imported from the Chinese kokakuchō tradition rather than indigenous Japanese folklore.


## Indigenous Perspectives

> ⚠️ **Modern English-language treatments sometimes reduce the ubume to a horror trope; the figure has long been understood as tragic and worthy of compassion within Japanese tradition.**

**Cultures**: Japanese (Heian through modern)

Ubume traditions express Japanese cosmological concern for the mother–child bond, salvation of the dead, and the dangers of liminal spaces (rivers, bridges, crossroads).
